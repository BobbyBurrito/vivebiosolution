Industrial Degreaser for CNC & Machine Shops: What Actually Matters?

Choosing a degreaser for a machine shop should be based on the cleaning process—not vague claims about being “professional” or “heavy duty.”

VIVE BIOSOLUTION · Updated July 31, 2026 · Practical commercial cleaning guide

Machine shops do not need an industrial degreaser because the label says “industrial.” They need a cleaner that fits the soils, surfaces and cleaning process actually present in the shop. That distinction matters because machining environments can contain way oil, hydraulic oil, grease, coolant residue, fingerprints and general shop soil—sometimes on the same piece of equipment.

Start With the Soil, Not the Marketing Claim

A cleaner that performs well on light oily film may not be the right choice for a heavy grease deposit. Likewise, a highly alkaline product that attacks difficult soil may create unnecessary compatibility concerns on sensitive surfaces. Before comparing products, identify what is being removed and where it is being removed from.

Typical machine-shop cleaning targets

Machine exteriors, guards, painted surfaces, work tables, maintenance areas, tools and compatible hard surfaces contaminated with oil, grease and general shop soil.

Low Foam Can Matter More Than Maximum Foam

Foam is not the same thing as cleaning power. In spray-and-wipe maintenance, excessive foam can increase wiping or rinsing and leave the operator spending more time removing cleaner after the soil has already been loosened. For a shop evaluating a degreaser, look at the complete process: application, soil release, wiping or rinsing, residue and the time required to return the surface to service.

Spray-and-Wipe vs. Rinse Cleaning

The right process depends on the surface and soil load. A localized oily film on a machine exterior may suit a controlled spray-and-wipe process. Heavier contamination or washable maintenance areas may benefit from rinsing. A product trial should replicate the way employees will actually clean—not an artificial demonstration that will never be repeated in production.

Surface Compatibility Should Be Part of the Trial

Machine shops contain painted equipment, plastics, elastomers, metals and other finishes. Do not assume that a product suitable for one surface is suitable for every surface in the building. Review the current label and SDS, follow manufacturer guidance, and test an inconspicuous area when compatibility is uncertain.

Concentrate or Ready-to-Use?

Concentrates can reduce cost per usable litre when dilution is controlled properly. Ready-to-use products reduce mixing decisions and can simplify operator use. The cheaper container is not necessarily the cheaper cleaning program. Compare the usable solution cost, labour, application method and consumption.

How to Run a Useful Degreaser Trial

  1. Choose a representative surface. Do not test only on the easiest soil in the shop.
  2. Record the soil. Note whether it is hydraulic oil, way oil, grease, coolant residue or mixed shop soil.
  3. Use the intended concentration. Record whether the cleaner is neat or diluted.
  4. Use the real cleaning method. Spray-and-wipe, mop, rinse or another normal process.
  5. Evaluate the finish. Look at soil removal, residue, foam, rinsing and operator effort.
  6. Check total cost. Include dilution, usage rate and labour—not only jug price.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does more foam mean an industrial degreaser cleans better?

No. Foam level and cleaning performance are separate characteristics. In some spray-and-wipe or rinse-sensitive applications, lower foam can make the cleaning process easier.

Should a machine shop use one degreaser on every surface?

Not automatically. Machine shops contain different metals, coatings, plastics and finishes. Follow the product guidance and confirm compatibility when the surface is uncertain.

What should we compare when testing two degreasers?

Compare soil removal, concentration, dwell time, wiping or rinsing required, residue, surface compatibility, operator effort and cost per usable solution—not only the purchase price.
